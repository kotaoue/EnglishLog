## テーマ: The "Tragedy of the Commons" and the Sustainability of Low Earth Orbit (LEO)

### 英文パッセージ

The proliferation of private satellite constellations in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) has ushered in a new era of global connectivity, yet it simultaneously presents a classic manifestation of the "Tragedy of the Commons." This economic theory posits that individuals, acting independently and rationally according to their own self-interest, behave contrary to the best interests of the whole group by depleting a shared resource. In the orbital context, the "resource" is not a finite physical commodity, but the safety and usability of the space environment itself.

As companies race to deploy thousands of satellites, the spatial density of objects increases exponentially, significantly elevating the risk of the "Kessler Syndrome." This hypothetical scenario describes a tipping point where the density of objects in LEO is high enough that collisions between objects could cause a cascade—each collision generating debris that increases the likelihood of further collisions. Such a chain reaction could eventually render specific orbital planes unusable for generations, effectively "locking" humanity out of space.

The crux of the issue lies in the lack of a robust, binding international regulatory framework. Currently, the orbital commons is characterized by "non-excludability," where no single nation can easily prevent another from launching assets. Furthermore, the short-term economic incentives for rapid deployment often outweigh the long-term, nebulous costs of environmental degradation. Addressing this requires a paradigm shift: viewing LEO not as an infinite frontier for exploitation, but as a fragile and finite ecosystem that demands collective stewardship and preemptive mitigation strategies.
