## テーマ: The Precautionary Principle and the Governance of Synthetic Biology

### 英文パッセージ

The rapid advancement of synthetic biology—the design and construction of new biological parts and systems—has ignited a profound debate regarding the appropriate regulatory framework for emerging biotechnologies. At the heart of this discourse lies the "Precautionary Principle," a philosophical and legal approach suggesting that if an action or policy has a suspected risk of causing harm to the public or the environment, the burden of proof that it is not harmful falls on those taking the action.

Critics of unfettered synthetic innovation argue that the complexity of living systems makes unintended consequences not just possible, but probable. For instance, the release of gene-drive organisms intended to eradicate malaria-carrying mosquitoes could inadvertently destabilize entire ecosystems through trophic cascades. In this context, the Precautionary Principle serves as a vital safeguard against irreversible ecological damage and "existential risks" that current biosafety protocols may fail to anticipate.

Conversely, proponents of the "Proactionary Principle" contend that an over-reliance on precaution stifles scientific progress and denies humanity the potential benefits of life-saving medical breakthroughs or sustainable biofuels. They argue that technological stagnation is itself a risk, as it leaves society vulnerable to existing problems that synthetic biology could otherwise solve. Striking a balance between these competing paradigms requires a nuanced approach to "Adaptive Governance," where regulations evolve in tandem with empirical evidence, ensuring that innovation proceeds with both ambition and humility.
