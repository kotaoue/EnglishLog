## テーマ: The "Overton Window" and the Boundaries of Public Discourse

### 英文パッセージ

The "Overton Window" is a concept used to describe the range of policies or ideas that the general public is willing to accept at any given time. Named after Joseph Overton, this model suggests that a politician’s success depends not on their individual brilliance, but on whether their ideas fit within this "window" of acceptable thought. Ideas inside the window are considered "sensible" or "popular," while those outside are seen as "radical" or "unthinkable."

The window is not fixed; it shifts over time as societal values change. This shift can happen naturally through cultural evolution or be intentionally moved by activists and thinkers. For example, ideas that were once considered extreme—such as women’s right to vote or remote working as a standard practice—eventually moved from the "unthinkable" category into the center of the window.

Critics of the concept argue that in the age of social media, the window is no longer a single frame. Instead, different groups have their own windows, leading to a polarized society where people cannot agree on what is "normal." Understanding the Overton Window helps us see how public opinion is shaped and how once-impossible ideas can eventually become law. The key takeaway is that for a policy to change, the conversation in society must often change first.
