## テーマ: The Concept of "Technological Somnambulism" and Autonomous Systems

### 英文パッセージ

"Technological somnambulism," a term famously coined by philosopher Langdon Winner, describes the tendency of societies to adopt new technologies without critically assessing their long-term social and political implications. We often treat technologies merely as neutral "tools"—instruments to be picked up or put down as we please. However, Winner argues that this instrumentalist view is dangerously reductive. Technologies are not just tools; they are structures that redefine human activity and social relations.

Consider the advent of the automobile. It was initially framed as a faster carriage, a mere convenience. Yet, its widespread adoption restructured urban geography, necessitated the construction of massive highway systems, and altered the very fabric of social interaction. This transformation occurred not by a conscious collective decision but as a byproduct of individual choices. In today's landscape, the rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) and algorithmic decision-making systems echoes this somnambulism. We focus on the immediate utility—efficiency in coding, drafting emails, or sorting data—while the underlying displacement of cognitive autonomy and the erosion of discursive sincerity remain peripheral concerns.

By the time we recognize that our technological "tools" have effectively rewritten our social contracts and cognitive habits, the infrastructure is already entrenched. Breaking free from technological somnambulism requires more than individual mindfulness; it demands a robust political philosophy of technology that questions how artifacts "have politics" and how they choreograph our lives.
