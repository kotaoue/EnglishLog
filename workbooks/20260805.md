## テーマ: The Challenges of Ocean Iron Fertilization as a Climate Mitigation Strategy

### 英文パッセージ

As the climate crisis intensifies, radical geoengineering strategies are increasingly under scrutiny. One such controversial approach is ocean iron fertilization (OIF). The premise is rooted in the biology of phytoplankton, microscopic organisms that form the base of the marine food web. Phytoplankton absorb atmospheric carbon dioxide through photosynthesis; when they die, they sink to the ocean floor, effectively sequestering carbon for centuries. In many "High-Nitrate, Low-Chlorophyll" regions, however, phytoplankton growth is limited by a lack of iron. By seeding these waters with iron sulfate, scientists aim to trigger massive blooms that could, in theory, offset a significant portion of anthropogenic emissions.

However, OIF is fraught with ecological and logistical uncertainties. While initial experiments have successfully induced blooms, the long-term efficiency of carbon sequestration remains dubious. A significant portion of the captured carbon may be recycled in the upper ocean rather than reaching the deep seabed. Furthermore, the unintended consequences are potentially devastating. Large-scale blooms could lead to "nutrient robbing," where nutrients are depleted in one area, depriving downstream ecosystems of essential resources. There is also the risk of promoting toxic algae species or creating oxygen-depleted "dead zones" as the excess organic matter decomposes.

The governance of OIF also presents a legal quagmire. International treaties like the London Convention currently restrict large-scale geoengineering experiments due to the "precautionary principle." Critics argue that commercializing OIF—for instance, through carbon credit markets—could incentivize reckless deployment before the systemic risks are fully understood. As we weigh the risks of climate inaction against the hazards of planetary-scale intervention, OIF remains a poignant symbol of the ethical dilemmas defining the Anthropocene.
