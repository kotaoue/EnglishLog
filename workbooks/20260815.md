## テーマ: The Concept of "Enshittification" and the Lifecycle of Digital Platforms

### 英文パッセージ

The term "enshittification," recently popularized by author Cory Doctorow, describes a perceived lifecycle that many dominant online platforms undergo. This sociological and economic phenomenon suggests that digital services experience a predictable decline in quality as they shift their strategic focus from user utility to shareholder value maximization. The process typically unfolds in three distinct stages, driven by the structural incentives of platform capitalism.

Initially, platforms offer immense value to attract a critical mass of users, often heavily subsidized by venture capital. During this phase, the user experience is paramount, and the service feels indispensable and "free." Once a massive user base is established, the platform pivots to attract business customers—such as advertisers or third-party sellers—by harvesting user data and offering favorable placement. At this point, the platform acts as a two-sided market, balancing the needs of users and businesses to ensure growth.

However, the final and most destructive stage occurs when the platform begins to "claw back" value from both users and businesses to satisfy its own bottom line. The user experience becomes increasingly degraded, cluttered with intrusive advertisements and algorithmic manipulations that prioritize high-margin content over relevance. Despite the declining quality, the platform remains dominant because it has become a "walled garden," where high switching costs and network effects make it prohibitively difficult for users to migrate elsewhere. This cycle highlights a fundamental tension in the digital age: the inherent conflict between sustainable product development and the predatory demands of short-term profit extraction. Modern regulators now face the daunting task of addressing these structural incentives that prioritize corporate rent-seeking over long-term public utility.
