## テーマ: The Lindy Effect and the Survivability of Non-Perishable Things

### 英文パッセージ

The Lindy Effect is a theoretical concept that posits a stark contrast between the life expectancy of biological entities and that of non-perishable things, such as ideas, books, or technologies. In the biological realm, aging is a relentless progression toward mortality; the older a human being gets, the shorter their remaining life expectancy becomes. However, for non-perishable items, the Lindy Effect suggests the exact opposite: the observed lifespan to date is proportional to the future life expectancy. In essence, every additional day an idea survives implies a longer remaining life.

This concept, popularized by Nassim Nicholas Taleb, suggests that time acts as a formidable filter for quality and relevance. If a book has been in print for fifty years, it is likely to be in print for another fifty. Conversely, a new bestseller that has been on the shelves for only two weeks might vanish from public consciousness within a month. This is because time identifies "robustness." Ideas that have survived centuries of scrutiny, cultural shifts, and technological upheavals have proven their inherent value, whereas new innovations often succumb to "neomania"—the fragile obsession with the new, which frequently leads to rapid obsolescence.

Applying the Lindy Effect to modern consumption suggests a counterintuitive strategy: to understand the future, one should look at the oldest surviving elements of the present. While we often equate technological advancement with progress, many digital tools are inherently fragile and destined for quick replacement. In contrast, the fundamental principles of logic, classical literature, and ancient architectural techniques remain relevant precisely because they have already withstood the test of time.
