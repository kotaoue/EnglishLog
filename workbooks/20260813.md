## テーマ: The "Jevons Paradox" and the Illusion of Resource Efficiency

### 英文パッセージ

In the discourse surrounding sustainable development, the Jevons Paradox stands as a sobering counter-narrative to the optimistic pursuit of resource efficiency. Named after the 19th-century economist William Stanley Jevons, the paradox posits that technological improvements that increase the efficiency with which a resource is used often lead to an increase in the total consumption of that resource, rather than a decrease. This phenomenon occurs because efficiency gains effectively lower the relative cost of utilizing the resource, thereby stimulating demand and fostering new applications that were previously economically unviable.

A classic historical example is Jevons’ observation regarding coal during the Industrial Revolution. As steam engines became more efficient, coal consumption did not plummet; instead, the enhanced performance made steam power applicable to a wider range of industries, ultimately skyrocketing national coal usage. In the modern era, a similar pattern is observable in information technology and transport. For instance, as lighting technologies transitioned from incandescent bulbs to highly efficient LEDs, the resulting cost reduction led to a proliferation of light installations, potentially offsetting the energy savings intended by the technological shift.

Critically, the Jevons Paradox underscores the limitations of purely technological solutions to environmental crises. It suggests that without systemic policy interventions—such as Pigouvian taxes or consumption caps—efficiency gains may inadvertently accelerate resource depletion. Consequently, addressing climate change and resource scarcity requires a paradigm shift that transcends technological optimism, necessitating a deeper interrogation of economic growth models and consumer behavior.
