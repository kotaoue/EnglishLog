## テーマ: The "Chinese Room" Argument and the Distinction Between Syntax and Semantics

### 英文パッセージ

Proposed by philosopher John Searle in 1980, the "Chinese Room" thought experiment serves as a robust critique of "Strong AI"—the view that a computer programmed with the right functional inputs and outputs would possess a mind and genuine understanding. Searle invites us to imagine a person, who knows no Chinese, locked in a room with a vast collection of English rules for manipulating Chinese symbols. When Chinese characters are passed into the room, the person follows the rules to produce appropriate Chinese responses. To an outside observer, the system appears to understand Chinese perfectly.

However, Searle argues that this appearance is deceptive. The person inside the room is merely performing "syntax"—the formal manipulation of symbols based on their shape and order—without any grasp of "semantics," which refers to the actual meaning or intentionality behind those symbols. While the system can simulate a conversation through sophisticated heuristics, it fails to instantiate true consciousness or comprehension. This distinction suggests that linguistic competence is not merely a matter of computational processing, but requires a biological or causal substrate that current digital architectures lack.

The implications of this argument are profound, especially in the era of Large Language Models (LLMs). Although modern AI can generate prose with startling fluency, the Chinese Room suggests that such output remains a sophisticated form of pattern matching, devoid of the internal mental states that characterize human cognition. Thus, the debate persists: is the gap between "simulation" and "reality" an unbridgeable chasm, or merely a temporary technological hurdle?
