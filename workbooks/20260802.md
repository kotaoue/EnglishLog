## テーマ: The Ethical Implications of De-extinction Technology

### 英文パッセージ

De-extinction—the process of resurrecting extinct species through advanced biotechnological methods like CRISPR gene editing and somatic cell nuclear transfer—has transitioned from the realm of science fiction to a tangible, albeit controversial, scientific pursuit. Proponents argue that bringing back keystone species, such as the woolly mammoth, could serve as a powerful tool for ecological restoration. For instance, the reintroduction of megafauna to the Siberian tundra could potentially mitigate permafrost melt by trampling insulating snow and stimulating the growth of carbon-sequestering grasses. This "Pleistocene Park" vision suggests that de-extinction is not merely a feat of vanity but a proactive strategy to combat climate change.

However, the ethical landscape is fraught with complexities. Critics contend that de-extinction may inadvertently diminish the urgency of contemporary conservation efforts, fostering a "moral hazard" where the permanence of extinction is no longer feared. Furthermore, the ecological niches once occupied by these species have evolved significantly; reintroducing a prehistoric animal into a modern ecosystem could result in unforeseen consequences, potentially displacing extant species or introducing novel pathogens. There are also profound animal welfare concerns regarding the surrogacy process and the quality of life for a creature born into a world it no longer recognizes. Ultimately, the pursuit of de-extinction necessitates a robust interdisciplinary framework that balances technological hubris with ecological humility, ensuring that the "resurrection" of the past does not come at the expense of the present biodiversity.
